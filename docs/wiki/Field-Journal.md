# The recovered journal

*The entries below belong to the story of this guide. The ink changes halfway through. The handwriting does not.*

## The western road

I took the western road because there was nothing on it worth stopping for.

The village had bread, a bed and someone willing to mend a broken buckle. Beyond it were birch trees, an old river crossing and three days of walking. I had travelled that way before. I remember being pleased that there was so little left to discover.

The bells started at dusk.

I turned back. No one came out to meet me. The people in the square were watching the far hill, where the colour had drained out of the clouds. One man had both hands over his ears, though the ringing had already stopped.

I asked what had happened.

He said, “It hasn't.”

I slept in the stable. In the morning I wrote that he had probably meant the storm was still coming. It is a reasonable explanation. I have kept it in the notebook because I would like at least one of those to remain reasonable.

## Purple

The first seam opened above the river. Thin enough at first that I mistook it for a fault in the light. Then it reached across the sky, and the hill behind it looked a long way farther off than it had any right to be.

I tried to measure the distance with arrows. This was a poor method. Three stayed in the air. I could still see them when I moved, arranged like the last few marks of a sentence someone had abandoned.

There was a figure on the ridge in my coat.

I have drawn the coat here because I know how that sounds. The repair under the left sleeve was visible. So was the pale strip where the bag strap had worn the shoulder. I raised a hand, and the figure finished lowering theirs.

I did not follow.

Later, in a patch of ground that belonged to somewhere else, I found a stone with a narrow, steady light in it. Near the stone the grass stayed where I put my feet. I carried it to the shelter.

It is on the table while I write this.

## Orange

Heat is easier to believe in. It hurts in the usual way, and there is a relief in that which I do not intend to defend.

The orange storm rose beyond the trees two evenings later. I watched it brighten, dim, and send a wave through the valley. The ground behind the wave held the colour of cooling iron. I waited until I thought it had cooled enough, then discovered how little I knew about colours.

My pickaxe came out of that valley changed. I had dropped it close to the column and gone back for it during the lull. The edge held longer afterward. I have tried to write the account plainly, because if I describe it as a gift I will have to decide who gave it.

That night I removed the soul lantern from the path outside the shelter. I had run past it once and watched blue fire answer blue fire down the slope. The creatures beyond the light turned towards me together.

I now walk the last stretch home.

## Blue

There was a blue storm by the old crossing. I was glad to see it. I thought I understood rain.

Snow had settled on one bank. On the other, water ran between blocks that had been dry ground when I left the village. I followed a pale patch of light with my eyes until lightning struck it. After that I looked down more often.

Near the centre, the weather loosened its grip. I stood there long enough to notice how tired my hands were. They had been holding the straps of my bag since the bells.

From inside, the storm seemed almost still. The world outside it was the thing moving past.

I thought about staying until it ended. Then I thought about how much I had wanted to believe that the ordinary colour meant ordinary weather, and left while I could still choose a direction.

## Teal

The quiet was complete enough to hear the leather of my boots bend.

I stopped at the edge and watched the column. Nothing moved towards me. Nothing needed to. When I stepped forward, light ran from the place my foot had landed towards the teal rings.

I waited. Tried another step. Waited again.

For a while this felt like an experiment I was conducting.

Then I spoke, very softly, to remind myself I could. The storm answered. I put my hand over my mouth and stayed that way until the dark thinned enough for me to see the path.

The entry I made that evening is only six words long: I had been taking turns with it.

I have no measurements for that sentence.

## The fourth column

I had arranged the pages by colour. Purple for distance. Orange for heat. Blue for weather. Teal for the quiet. It made the notebook useful. I could find things quickly. I began to carry it with the confidence of someone who had mistaken filing for understanding.

Then two storms met.

I watched their light move together over a hill I knew. For several minutes I could still point to each colour and name what I expected. After that I stopped pointing.

The shelter was where I had left it. The stone on the table was steady. I sat beside it with the notebook open and noticed that I had left a blank page after the fourth colour.

I do not remember deciding to leave it blank.

Tomorrow I will go back to the village. I will ask whether the bells rang before the first storm, or before anyone remembered seeing one. It matters to me now that I get the question right.

If someone finds this before I return, the stone can be moved. Take it with you. The notebook is less important.

Leave the last page as it is.
